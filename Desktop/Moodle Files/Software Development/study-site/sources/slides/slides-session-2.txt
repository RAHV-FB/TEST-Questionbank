Where Waterfall genuinely works
Waterfall assumes the requirements are known in advance and stable.
When that is true, it works.
Bridges, buildings, aircraft, cars. The requirements really are known up front, changing
them mid-build really is ruinously expensive, and regulation demands the documentation.
Projects where predictability matters more than speed: you know early roughly what the
finished thing looks like and what it costs.
Contracts with a fixed scope and a fixed price.
Alberto Cámara | Software Development for Business | Session 2

===== [slides-session-2.pdf] page 7 =====
Why it fails for software
Software breaks all three of Waterfall's assumptions:
Requirements change. The customer sees the first version and understands what they
actually wanted. This is normal, not a failure of analysis.
Feedback arrives too late. Testing is done near the end, so a wrong assumption early in
the project is only found close to the end.
Integration risk piles up. Four people build four parts in isolation; they are first combined
at the end, and then nothing fits.
Alberto Cámara | Software Development for Business | Session 2

Agile

===== [slides-session-2.pdf] page 10 =====
What Agile is
Formalised in 2001, by practitioners reacting to Waterfall's failures on software
Iterative, incremental, adaptable: build a small working slice, show it, learn, adjust
Delivers working software frequently — weeks, not years
Plan
Build
Review with user
Learn
The same lifecycle phases still happen — they just happen in every cycle, on a small
slice, instead of once across the whole project.

Workshop: plan, then re-plan

===== [slides-session-2.pdf] page 17 =====
What to do
Full brief on Moodle. In short:
Part 1 — as Waterfall (15 min)
Take a simple project idea. Plan it in five
phases, start to finish. Prepare a 2-minute
pitch of the plan.
Part 2 — as Agile (15 min)
Same idea. Re-plan as short iterations.
What do you build first? What changed?
What did the change cost you?
Then a few groups present both plans and we compare.
